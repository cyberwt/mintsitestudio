GRE vocabulary: 121 words

Words that keep showing up in text completion and sentence equivalence. Flip a card, say whether you knew it, and the deck brings back the ones you’re still learning.

How to learn GRE words

Learn words in context, not as isolated definitions: the GRE tests whether a word fits a sentence’s logic. Say each example sentence out loud, then try writing your own. Ten new words a day, reviewed the next day and again a week later, beats cramming two hundred the week before the test.

Notice the pairs: sentence equivalence answers are near-synonyms (soporific and monotonous, aloof and reserved), and the wrong options often include a second synonym pair with the opposite meaning. Try them in the Verbal practice questions.

The full word list

  • abate verb to lessen in intensity. The storm abated by morning, and the ferries ran again.
  • aberrant adj. departing from what is normal or expected. One aberrant result was not enough to overturn the study.
  • abstruse adj. hard to understand; obscure. The lecture on tax law was too abstruse for most of the audience.
  • acerbic adj. sharp and biting in tone. Her acerbic reviews made restaurant owners nervous.
  • admonish verb to warn or reprimand mildly. The coach admonished the players for arriving late.
  • aesthetic adj. concerned with beauty or taste. The redesign was driven by aesthetic rather than practical concerns.
  • alacrity noun cheerful eagerness. She accepted the offer with alacrity.
  • ambivalent adj. having mixed feelings. He was ambivalent about moving: excited by the job, sad to leave friends.
  • ameliorate verb to make a bad situation better. New bus lanes helped ameliorate the downtown traffic.
  • anachronism noun something placed in the wrong historical period. A wristwatch in a film about ancient Rome is an anachronism.
  • anomaly noun something that deviates from the norm. The warm week in January was an anomaly.
  • antipathy noun a strong dislike. His antipathy toward meetings was well known.
  • apathy noun lack of interest or concern. Voter apathy kept turnout low.
  • arcane adj. known or understood by very few. The rules of the old card game were arcane.
  • arduous adj. requiring great effort. The climb was arduous but the view was worth it.
  • articulate adj. able to express ideas clearly. An articulate speaker, she made the policy easy to follow.
  • ascetic adj. practicing strict self-denial. The monk lived an ascetic life with few possessions.
  • assuage verb to ease or calm (a feeling). The refund did little to assuage the customers' anger.
  • audacious adj. boldly daring. It was an audacious plan to cross the desert on foot.
  • austere adj. severely plain; without comfort. The office was austere: a desk, a chair and a lamp.
  • banal adj. dull from overuse; unoriginal. The movie's dialogue was banal and predictable.
  • belie verb to give a false impression of. His calm voice belied his nervousness.
  • bolster verb to support or strengthen. New evidence bolstered the defense's case.
  • bombastic adj. pompous and inflated in language. The bombastic speech promised everything and explained nothing.
  • burgeon verb to grow rapidly. The town's burgeoning population strained its schools.
  • cajole verb to persuade by flattery or coaxing. She cajoled her brother into helping her move.
  • capricious adj. changing suddenly and unpredictably. The capricious weather ruined our picnic plans twice.
  • castigate verb to criticize severely. The editorial castigated the council for wasting money.
  • catalyst noun something that causes or speeds up change. The strike was a catalyst for new labor laws.
  • caustic adj. sarcastic in a hurtful way. His caustic remarks left the team demoralized.
  • censure verb to express formal disapproval of. The board censured the director for the leak.
  • chicanery noun trickery used to deceive. The audit uncovered years of financial chicanery.
  • circumspect adj. cautious; careful to consider risks. Be circumspect about what you post online.
  • cogent adj. clear and convincing. She made a cogent case for the new budget.
  • complacent adj. self-satisfied and unaware of danger. After years of success, the company grew complacent.
  • conciliatory adj. intended to make peace. He sent a conciliatory email after the argument.
  • condone verb to accept or allow (wrongdoing). The school does not condone cheating of any kind.
  • convoluted adj. overly complicated and hard to follow. The instructions were so convoluted that no one could assemble the shelf.
  • corroborate verb to confirm with evidence. Two witnesses corroborated her account.
  • credulous adj. too ready to believe things. Credulous readers shared the fake story widely.
  • cursory adj. hasty and not thorough. A cursory glance missed the error on page two.
  • dearth noun a scarcity or lack. There is a dearth of affordable housing in the city.
  • deference noun respectful submission to another's judgment. In deference to her experience, the team followed her plan.
  • denigrate verb to criticize unfairly; belittle. Don't denigrate other people's work to make yours look better.
  • diatribe noun a bitter, abusive verbal attack. The post turned into a long diatribe against his former employer.
  • didactic adj. intended to teach, often in a preachy way. The novel's didactic tone put off some readers.
  • diffident adj. shy and lacking self-confidence. The diffident student rarely spoke in class.
  • dilatory adj. slow; tending to delay. Dilatory tactics pushed the vote back a month.
  • disparate adj. fundamentally different. The book brings together disparate ideas from biology and economics.
  • dissemble verb to hide one's true motives or feelings. He dissembled when asked where the money went.
  • dogmatic adj. rigidly insisting one's views are right. A dogmatic manager rarely listens to new ideas.
  • eclectic adj. drawn from a wide range of sources. Her eclectic playlist mixes jazz, folk and hip-hop.
  • efficacy noun the ability to produce the intended result. Trials tested the efficacy of the new drug.
  • elucidate verb to make clear; explain. The teacher used a diagram to elucidate the process.
  • enervate verb to drain of energy. The long, humid afternoon enervated the hikers.
  • ephemeral adj. lasting a very short time. Fame on social media is often ephemeral.
  • equivocate verb to speak vaguely to avoid committing. When asked about layoffs, the CEO equivocated.
  • erudite adj. having great knowledge from study. The erudite professor could quote Latin poets from memory.
  • esoteric adj. understood only by a small group. The forum is full of esoteric debates about vintage cameras.
  • exacerbate verb to make a problem worse. Skipping sleep only exacerbated his stress.
  • exculpate verb to clear from blame. The video exculpated the driver.
  • exigent adj. requiring immediate action; urgent. Exigent circumstances allowed the police to enter.
  • extol verb to praise highly. Reviewers extolled the restaurant's simple menu.
  • fastidious adj. very attentive to detail and cleanliness. A fastidious editor, she caught every misplaced comma.
  • fervent adj. showing intense feeling. He was a fervent supporter of the local team.
  • florid adj. overly ornate; flowery. The florid prose buried a simple story.
  • foment verb to stir up (trouble). The rumors fomented distrust among the staff.
  • frugal adj. careful with money; thrifty. A frugal shopper, she compared prices at three stores.
  • garrulous adj. excessively talkative. The garrulous driver talked for the entire trip.
  • gregarious adj. sociable; fond of company. Gregarious by nature, he knew everyone on the block.
  • hackneyed adj. overused and unoriginal. The speech was full of hackneyed phrases.
  • harangue noun a long, aggressive lecture. The manager's harangue about deadlines lasted an hour.
  • hegemony noun dominance of one group over others. The empire's hegemony lasted two centuries.
  • iconoclast noun a person who attacks cherished beliefs. An iconoclast, the director ignored every rule of the genre.
  • impassive adj. showing no emotion. The judge remained impassive as the verdict was read.
  • impetuous adj. acting quickly without thought. An impetuous purchase left him without rent money.
  • inchoate adj. just begun; not fully formed. The plan was still inchoate, a few notes on a napkin.
  • indolent adj. lazy; avoiding effort. The indolent cat slept through the whole afternoon.
  • ingenuous adj. innocent and unsuspecting. Her ingenuous questions revealed she had never seen a contract.
  • intransigent adj. refusing to compromise. Talks stalled because both sides were intransigent.
  • laconic adj. using very few words. His laconic reply was simply, “No.”
  • laudable adj. deserving praise. Her goal of reading every day is laudable.
  • lucid adj. clear and easy to understand. The manual gives lucid step-by-step instructions.
  • magnanimous adj. generous and forgiving, especially to a rival. In a magnanimous speech, the winner thanked her opponent.
  • malleable adj. easily shaped or influenced. Young children's opinions are malleable.
  • maverick noun an independent thinker who goes their own way. A political maverick, she often voted against her party.
  • meticulous adj. showing great attention to detail. His meticulous notes made the report easy to write.
  • misanthrope noun a person who dislikes people in general. The novel's hero is a misanthrope who lives alone on an island.
  • mitigate verb to make less severe. Planting trees can mitigate flooding.
  • mollify verb to soothe or calm someone's anger. A free dessert mollified the annoyed diners.
  • morose adj. gloomy and sullen. He was morose for days after the loss.
  • obdurate adj. stubbornly refusing to change. The obdurate landlord would not fix the heater.
  • obsequious adj. excessively eager to please. The obsequious assistant agreed with everything the boss said.
  • obviate verb to remove (a need or difficulty). Online forms obviate the need to visit the office.
  • officious adj. meddlesome; too eager to give orders. An officious guard demanded to see our tickets three times.
  • onerous adj. burdensome. The new rules put onerous paperwork on small businesses.
  • opprobrium noun harsh public criticism. The company faced opprobrium after the spill.
  • ostentatious adj. designed to impress; showy. The ostentatious car had gold-plated wheels.
  • paragon noun a perfect example. She was a paragon of patience with new hires.
  • partisan adj. strongly favoring one side. The debate turned bitterly partisan.
  • paucity noun smallness of number or amount. The paucity of evidence made the case hard to prove.
  • pedantic adj. overly concerned with minor rules or details. His pedantic corrections annoyed the whole group.
  • perfunctory adj. done with minimal effort or care. He gave a perfunctory nod and kept typing.
  • placate verb to calm by making concessions. The airline offered vouchers to placate stranded travelers.
  • pragmatic adj. practical rather than idealistic. A pragmatic choice, the used car was cheap to run.
  • precipitate verb to cause to happen suddenly. The scandal precipitated the minister's resignation.
  • prevaricate verb to avoid the truth; speak evasively. Asked directly, the witness prevaricated.
  • profligate adj. recklessly wasteful. Profligate spending emptied the fund in a year.
  • prosaic adj. ordinary and unimaginative. The explanation was prosaic: the light had a loose wire.
  • quiescent adj. inactive or dormant. The volcano has been quiescent for centuries.
  • recalcitrant adj. stubbornly uncooperative. The recalcitrant mule refused to move.
  • reticent adj. reluctant to share thoughts or feelings. She was reticent about her past.
  • sanguine adj. optimistic, especially in a hard situation. Despite the delays, the engineers were sanguine about the launch.
  • soporific adj. tending to cause sleep. The soporific hum of the fan put him to sleep.
  • spurious adj. false or fake, though seeming genuine. The ad made spurious claims about curing colds.
  • stoic adj. enduring hardship without complaint. She remained stoic through months of recovery.
  • tenuous adj. very weak or slight. The link between the two events is tenuous at best.
  • torpor noun a state of sluggish inactivity. After the big meal, the family sank into torpor.
  • vacillate verb to waver between options. He vacillated between the two job offers for weeks.
  • venerate verb to regard with great respect. The villagers venerate their elders.
  • zealous adj. showing great energy for a cause. Zealous volunteers knocked on every door in the district.
