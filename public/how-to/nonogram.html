How to solve nonograms

A nonogram (also called a picross or griddler) hides a picture in a grid. The numbers beside each row and above each column tell you how the filled squares are grouped. You never need to guess: each line gives away a few squares, and those squares unlock the lines that cross them.

The rules

  • Each clue lists the lengths of the filled runs in that line, in order. “3 1” means a run of three, at least one empty square, then a run of one.
  • Runs in the same line are separated by at least one empty square.
  • When every row and column matches its clue, the picture is complete.

1. Count the overlap

This is the technique that starts almost every puzzle. Take a 10-square row with the clue 7. Push the run as far left as it can go and it covers squares 1–7. Push it right and it covers 4–10. Squares 4 to 7 are filled either way, so fill them now.

Quick rule: for a single run of length k in a line of n squares, the middle 2k − n squares are always filled. For several runs, add the runs plus one gap between each, then do the same subtraction: the clue “4 3” in 10 squares needs 4 + 1 + 3 = 8, which leaves 2 spare squares, so any run longer than 2 has sure squares. The 4 gets two (squares 3–4) and the 3 gets one (square 8).

2. Fill the full lines

A clue of 10 in a 10-wide grid fills the whole line. A clue like “5 4” also fills the line, because 5 + 1 + 4 = 10 leaves no room to move. Do these first.

3. Mark what must be empty

Empty squares are as useful as filled ones. When a run is complete, mark the squares on both sides with ✕. When a line’s clues are all placed, cross out the rest of the line.

4. Push from the edges

If a filled square sits near the edge, the first run must include it. With a clue of 3 and square 2 filled, the run covers square 2 and can reach no further right than square 4, so anything past square 4 that belongs to this run cannot be filled. Edges and crossed-out squares act like walls you can push against.

5. Cross-check every new square

Each square you fill or cross out belongs to one row and one column. After working a row, look at the columns you just touched: a single new square often lets you run the overlap count again with less room, and the grid unravels from there.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the ✕ marks. Without them you lose track of where runs can’t go. Switch to ✕ mode often.
  • Mixing up the order. “1 3” and “3 1” are different lines: the runs appear in the clue’s order, left to right or top to bottom.
  • Guessing to break a stall. One wrong square spreads through the whole grid. Every Mint Nonogram is checked by a line-by-line solver, so a logical step always exists.
